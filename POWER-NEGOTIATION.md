# How to Negotiate Your Salary Like a Pro (Step-by-Step Scripts)

> Read this file BEFORE you get an offer. Not during the call. Not after you've already accepted. Before. Because once you say "yes" on the call, the negotiation is over.

---

## The Mindset First

Negotiation is not rude. It is not greedy. It is not ungrateful.

It is expected. Hiring managers budget for it. Companies make their first offer lower than they're willing to pay precisely because they expect candidates to negotiate. When you don't negotiate, you leave money on the table that was sitting there with your name on it.

You negotiated 12 weeks of hard work to be in this position. Spend 5 minutes negotiating the compensation for years of your career.

---

## Rule 1: Never Give Your Number First

When they ask "What's your expected CTC?" — do not give a number.

**Script:**
> "I'm flexible on compensation. I'm more focused on finding the right team and the right growth opportunity. Could you share the budget range for this role?"

Why: Whoever gives the first number sets the anchor. If you say 4 LPA and they had 6 LPA budgeted, you've lost 2 LPA forever. If they say 5-7 LPA, you now know the floor.

---

## Rule 2: If They Insist, Give a Range

If they push back ("We need a number to proceed") — give a range with your target at the low end.

**Script:**
> "Based on my research for React developers at [city] startups, I'm looking at a 5-8 LPA range, but I'm open to discussing based on the total opportunity — growth, learning, team."

Why: The low end of your range becomes their reference point for a "reasonable" offer. Put your target there, not in the middle.

---

## Rule 3: Never Accept on the Call

When they make an offer — any offer, even a great one — do not say yes on the call.

**Script:**
> "Thank you so much! I'm genuinely excited about this opportunity and the team. Could I take 2-3 days to review the details before getting back to you?"

They will always say yes. No legitimate company will rescind an offer because you took 48 hours to think.

Those 48 hours are for: researching the market rate one more time, deciding if you want to counter, thinking about the role clearly without the excitement of the moment, and getting any competing offers into play if you have them.

---

## Rule 4: Counter If The Offer Is Below Your Range

**Script:**
> "Thank you for the offer of X LPA. I'm genuinely excited about this role and the team at [Company]. Based on my skills in React and TypeScript, my three deployed projects, and the market rate for frontend developers in [city], I was hoping for something closer to Y LPA. Is there flexibility in the budget?"

Keep it simple. One counter. One number. No long justification. Let them respond.

What they'll say:
- "Yes, we can do Y" → Accept
- "We can do Z (between X and Y)" → Decide if that's acceptable. It usually is.
- "X is our final budget" → Go to Rule 5

---

## Rule 5: If They Can't Move on Base Salary, Negotiate Everything Else

Base salary is not the only lever. If they're stuck on it:

**Script:**
> "I understand the budget constraints — I appreciate you being transparent about that. Would it be possible to discuss: a performance review at 6 months instead of 12, a signing bonus, or a learning and certification budget?"

Other things you can negotiate:
- Work-from-home days per week
- Equipment (laptop/monitor)
- Notice period flexibility
- Start date
- Role title (Junior vs Associate — matters for your next job search)

Even if they say no to all of it, you've shown professionalism, not desperation.

---

## Rule 6: If You Have Multiple Offers, Use Them

Only do this if it's true. Never fabricate competing offers — it will backfire.

**Script:**
> "I want to be transparent with you — I'm also in discussions with another company in the [X] LPA range. I genuinely prefer [this company] for [specific reason], and I'd love to make this work. Is there any flexibility to get closer to that range?"

This works because: it creates urgency, it's verifiable honesty (they respect that), and it gives them a specific number to respond to.

---

## When to Accept

Accept if ALL of these are true:
- Offer is 4.5+ LPA at a company with good learning scope
- The role is actual frontend development (not support or testing disguised as "developer")
- The company has decent Glassdoor reviews (read 10-15, not just the best ones)
- You get the offer in writing before your first day

**Remember:** Your first job's primary value is the experience and the resume line. After 1-2 years of real work, your second job can pay 2-3x more. Don't let the perfect salary be the enemy of a good start.

---

## When to Reject

Reject if ANY of these are true:
- Offer is below 3.5 LPA (you can do better; don't start low)
- The role description changed after you accepted (red flag for integrity)
- The role is not actual development — testing, support, data entry labeled as "developer"
- The company has a pattern of terrible Glassdoor reviews about unpaid overtime, no growth, or toxic management
- They refuse to give you an offer letter (verbal offers don't count — always get it in writing)

---

## Red Flags in Offer Calls

Watch for these. Any one of them is a reason to slow down and ask hard questions:

**"We'll finalize your role after you join."**
This means they'll put you wherever they need bodies. You agreed to "Frontend Developer" — make sure that's in the letter.

**No offer letter, only verbal.**
Not acceptable. You need the CTC, role title, joining date, and terms in writing. Ask: "When can I expect the formal offer letter?" If they hesitate, something is wrong.

**Bond clause: 2+ year commitment with financial penalty.**
Not illegal, but evaluate carefully. 2 years is long. 1 year at a good company is reasonable. Read the penalty amount — some bonds have 1-3 lakh rupee clauses. Ask if it can be negotiated down or removed.

**Salary split: part in hand, part as reimbursements.**
Your CTC may look higher than your in-hand salary because of expense reimbursements that require receipts. Ask for the in-hand monthly number explicitly.

**"We'll increase your salary after your 3-month probation."**
Sometimes true. Often not. Ask for it in writing — "subject to satisfactory performance review at 3 months, salary will be revised to X LPA." If they won't put it in writing, assume it won't happen.

## The Scripts in One Place

**When asked for expected CTC:**
> "I'm flexible — could you share the budgeted range for this role?"

**When pushed for a number:**
> "Based on market research, I'm looking at 5-8 LPA, open to discussing."

**When receiving an offer:**
> "Thank you! I'm excited. Could I take 2-3 days to review the details?"

**When countering:**
> "I was hoping for something closer to Y LPA — is there flexibility?"

**When base is fixed:**
> "Could we discuss a 6-month review, signing bonus, or learning budget?"

**When you have another offer:**
> "I'm in discussions elsewhere at X LPA — I'd love to make this work first."
